Your brain cancels your own tickles
before you feel them.
But how?
Every time you move,
your brain sends a copy of the order
to the cerebellum,
at the back of your head.
It predicts what the touch will feel like,
then turns the feeling down.
No surprise, no tickle.
When someone else tickles you,
there's no prediction.
Your brain can't guess where or when,
so it hits full blast.
Scientists tested this with a robot.
People moved a handle,
and a robot brushed their palm.
Moving it themselves? Barely ticklish.
Then the robot added a delay
of just two tenths of a second.
Suddenly, it felt more ticklish.
The prediction was off,
so the brain couldn't cancel it.
So your own brain blocks the tickle.
Can you find a way to outsmart it?
