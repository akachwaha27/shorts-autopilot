So why did chocolate stop being money,

This is the story of chocolate.
It starts with a tree in the rainforest.
The cacao tree grows big, football-shaped pods,
right on its trunk.
Inside each pod are thirty to fifty seeds,
wrapped in sweet white pulp.
Those seeds are cocoa beans.
Scientists found traces of cacao in pots
from the Amazon in Ecuador,
about five thousand three hundred years old.
Later, the Olmec and the Maya
turned the beans into a drink.
They roasted them, ground them into paste,
and mixed in water, chili, and cornmeal.
Then they poured it from high up,
from one cup into another,
to whip up a thick foam on top.
And here's the thing.
It wasn't sweet at all.
It was bitter, spicy, and very special.
The Aztecs loved it so much
they used the beans as money.
One record from the fifteen hundreds lists prices.
A tomato cost one bean.
An avocado, three beans.
A turkey hen, one hundred.
Some people even made fake beans,
by stuffing empty shells with mud.
Then, in the fifteen hundreds,
Spanish explorers carried cacao back across the ocean.
The Spanish court tried the bitter drink,
and did something new.
They added sugar,
plus cinnamon and vanilla.
Suddenly, Europe was hooked.
Fancy chocolate houses opened in London,
where rich people sipped it hot.
But for almost three hundred years,
chocolate was still only a drink.
Here's why.
A cocoa bean is about half fat,
called cocoa butter.
Grind the beans, and you get a thick, greasy paste.
In eighteen twenty eight,
a Dutch chemist named Coenraad van Houten
built a press that squeezed out most of that butter.
What was left became a dry cake,
that could be crushed into cocoa powder.
Then in eighteen forty seven,
an English company called Fry's flipped the idea.
They mixed cocoa powder and sugar
with extra melted cocoa butter,
and poured it into a mold.
It set hard.
The first modern chocolate bar.
It was still gritty, and a bit bitter.
Two Swiss makers fixed that.
In eighteen seventy five,
Daniel Peter added milk,
using milk powder from his neighbor, Henri Nestlé.
Milk chocolate was born.
Then in eighteen seventy nine,
Rodolphe Lindt built a machine called a conche.
It rolls warm chocolate back and forth for hours,
grinding every bit smaller and smaller,
until it turns silky smooth.
So what happens today?
Farmers cut open the pods
and let the beans ferment for about a week.
That's where most of the chocolate flavor comes from.
Then the beans are dried, roasted, and cracked.
It takes around four hundred beans
to make one pound of chocolate.
And the best trick is in the butter.
Cocoa butter melts at about thirty four degrees Celsius,
just below the heat inside your mouth.
That's why a bar stays solid in your hand,
but melts on your tongue.
