You were born with nearly three hundred bones.
So where did almost a hundred of them go?
The answer is that your skeleton is alive,
and it's rebuilding itself right now.
Most of us picture bones as dry white sticks,
like the plastic skeleton in a science class.
But the bones inside you have blood vessels,
nerves, and billions of busy cells.
So what's actually going on in there?
Let's paint it out, one bone at a time.
Chapter one. More bones than you think.
A grown-up has about two hundred and six bones.
But a newborn has close to three hundred.
The missing ones didn't disappear.
They fused together as you grew.
A baby's skull is a set of soft plates,
with gaps that slowly close and lock.
Five little bones at the bottom of your spine
join up into one solid piece.
Your hands and feet hold one hundred and six bones,
more than half of your whole skeleton.
Chapter two. What bone is made of.
Bone is a mix of two very different things.
The first is collagen,
a stretchy protein, a bit like rope.
The second is a hard mineral made of calcium,
packed between the ropes like tiny bricks.
Rope alone would bend, bricks alone would shatter.
Together, you get something tough and a little springy.
And your bones are also a bank.
They store about ninety nine percent of your body's calcium.
When your blood runs low,
your bones pay some out,
so your heart and muscles keep working.
Chapter three. The demolition crew.
Deep inside every bone, two kinds of cells take turns.
Osteoclasts dissolve tiny pockets of old bone.
Then osteoblasts move in,
and fill the hole with fresh new bone.
Dig, then fill. Dig, then fill.
In adults, around ten percent of the skeleton
gets rebuilt every year.
That works out to roughly a brand new skeleton
about every ten years.
This same crew is why a broken bone can heal.
First a soft bridge forms across the crack,
then the crew slowly turns it into hard bone again.
Chapter four. The blood factory.
Now look inside a big bone, like your thigh bone.
The middle is filled with soft marrow,
and that marrow is a factory.
It makes your red blood cells,
the white cells that fight germs,
and the platelets that plug up cuts.
How many? Scientists estimate
around two hundred billion red blood cells,
every single day.
Chapter five. Use it or lose it.
Your bones listen to how you use them.
Push on them, and they build up.
Rest them for too long, and they let go.
Astronauts floating in space can lose
about one percent of the bone in their hips each month.
Meanwhile, studies of tennis players find
the bones in their racket arm are thicker
than the bones in their other arm.
So every jump, every run, every climb up the stairs
is a little message telling your skeleton to stay strong.
So no, your skeleton isn't a dead frame.
It's a living, growing part of you,
quietly rebuilding itself, one tiny pocket at a time.
Next time, your liver,
the organ that does hundreds of jobs at once.
