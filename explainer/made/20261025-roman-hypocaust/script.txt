How did the Romans heat their floors,
without a single wire?
Two thousand years ago,
in the middle of winter,
a Roman could walk across a stone floor barefoot,
and it would be warm.
The trick was hiding under their feet.
It's called a hypocaust,
which means "heat from below."
The Roman writer Vitruvius
credits a businessman named Sergius Orata,
around 95 BC.
Orata got rich farming oysters,
and he sold wealthy Romans on heated "hanging baths."
Soon, no fancy villa was complete without one.
So how did it work?
Start with a furnace,
called the praefurnium,
built just outside the room, a little lower down.
Slaves fed it wood all day.
Now, instead of laying the floor on the ground,
the Romans lifted it up.
They built short stacks of square bricks,
called pilae,
row after row.
Vitruvius says two feet tall.
On top went big two-foot tiles,
then a thick layer of concrete,
then the floor you'd walk on.
That left a hollow space underneath,
like a crawl space made for smoke.
The fire's hot air and smoke
rushed into that gap,
swirled between the pillars,
and soaked heat into the floor above.
But it didn't stop there.
Hollow clay pipes, called tubuli,
ran up inside the walls.
Hot air climbed through them,
warming the walls too,
then escaped through vents near the roof.
So the whole room became a radiator.
Here's a detail Vitruvius was oddly specific about.
The floor underneath should slope toward the furnace,
so if you threw a ball in,
it couldn't stop inside.
It would roll right back to the fire.
Romans also lined up their rooms by heat.
The caldarium, the hot room,
sat closest to the furnace.
Next came the warm room,
the tepidarium.
And the cold plunge pool, the frigidarium,
sat farthest away.
So, how hot did it get?
In modern tests on rebuilt Roman baths,
the floors reached twenty to fifty degrees Celsius.
Hot enough that bathers wore wooden-soled sandals.
The hot room's air sat around thirty-two degrees,
thick with steam.
And all of it ran on wood.
The giant Baths of Caracalla in Rome
reportedly burned around ten tons of wood,
every single day.
When the empire fell apart,
so did the skills and the labor
that kept these furnaces burning.
For centuries,
much of Europe went back to smoky open fires.
But the idea never died.
In Korea, the ondol system
runs fire smoke under the floor in a very similar way.
And today's underfloor heating
does exactly what the Romans did:
warm the floor,
and let the heat rise.
Just with pipes instead of pillars,
and nobody shoveling wood.
Would you trade your radiator
for a Roman floor?
