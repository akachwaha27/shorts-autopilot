Your stomach is full of acid strong enough to dissolve metal.
So why doesn't it dissolve you?
The secret is a wall that rebuilds itself every few days.
Every day it breaks down steak, cheese and crunchy vegetables,
but it never eats the walls holding it all in.
For a long time, nobody knew what a stomach actually did.
Some thought it just ground food up like a mill.
Then in the 1700s, an Italian scientist named Lazzaro Spallanzani
pulled stomach juice out on a sponge tied to a string.
And he showed that this juice could dissolve meat
even outside the body.
So the stomach wasn't just a grinder.
It was a chemistry lab.
And later, the French scientist Claude Bernard noticed something odd.
The stomach wall acts as if it were made of porcelain.
So what's inside this lab?
Your stomach lining is covered in millions of tiny pits.
Deep down in them, special cells called parietal cells
pump out hydrochloric acid.
That gives your stomach juice a pH of about one and a half to three and a half.
At its strongest, it's even more acidic than lemon juice.
Strong enough to dissolve a piece of zinc.
Next door, chief cells make an enzyme called pepsin,
which chops proteins into little pieces.
And here's the clever part.
Pepsin is made switched off.
It leaves the cell as a harmless version called pepsinogen,
and only the acid outside can switch it on.
So the knife is never sharp inside the cell that makes it.
But that still leaves the stomach wall sitting in acid.
That's where defense number one comes in: slime.
The lining is coated in a thick, sticky layer of mucus.
And that mucus is loaded with bicarbonate,
the same kind of stuff as baking soda.
Acid trickles in from one side, bicarbonate soaks it up from the other.
In lab studies, the acid on one side of the mucus was strong,
but right against the cells, it was close to neutral.
Under the slime, the cells are zipped together with tight junctions,
so acid can't leak between them.
Defense number two is even wilder.
Your stomach just keeps replacing itself.
The surface lining is completely renewed every three to six days.
One old estimate says about half a million cells get shed every minute.
So even if a few cells get burned, fresh ones are already on the way.
And this stomach is built to stretch.
Empty, it's about the size of your fist.
But it can hold up to four liters,
more than seventy-five times its empty size.
Every twenty seconds or so, a wave of muscle squeezes the mix,
and two to four hours after a meal, it's pushed on into your gut.
So what can break the shield?
A corkscrew-shaped bacterium called H. pylori
can burrow into the mucus and weaken it.
That's why some sores in the stomach lining are now treated as infections.
So the next time you eat a big meal,
remember there's a pool of acid in your belly,
and a slimy, self-repairing wall that never lets it win.
