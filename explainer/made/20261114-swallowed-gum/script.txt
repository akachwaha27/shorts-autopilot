Swallowed gum never stays in you for seven years.
It's actually gone in a few days.
So where did the seven-year story come from?
And what actually happens to gum inside you?
Let's follow one swallowed piece, all the way through.
Chapter one. The seven-year myth.
Almost everyone hears it as a kid.
Swallow your gum, and it sits in your stomach for seven years.
Nobody really knows who started it.
But there's a grain of truth hiding inside.
Your body can't digest gum. Not properly.
It just doesn't need to.
Chapter two. What gum is made of.
People have chewed gummy stuff for thousands of years.
In Denmark, scientists found a lump of chewed birch tar
about five thousand seven hundred years old.
It still held the chewer's DNA, and her tooth marks.
Far away, the Maya chewed chicle,
a milky sap tapped from the sapodilla tree.
In the eighteen seventies, an inventor named Thomas Adams
started selling chicle as chewing gum in America.
Today most gum starts with a gum base,
made of rubbery, food-grade polymers.
Then sweetener, softeners and flavour get mixed in.
Chapter three. Why you can't digest it.
Digestion is mostly chemistry.
Your spit, stomach and gut make enzymes,
tiny molecular scissors that snip food into pieces.
One kind cuts starch, one cuts protein, and one cuts fat.
The sugar and flavour in gum dissolve away while you chew.
But the gum base is made of long, tangled chains
that none of those scissors can cut.
Even your stomach acid barely touches it.
So the rubbery part stays rubbery, the whole way through.
And honestly, that's fine.
Your body handles stuff it can't digest every single day.
Chapter four. The journey out.
Once you swallow, the gum slides down your food pipe in seconds.
In your stomach, strong muscle walls churn it with your lunch.
Then it squeezes through a little gate at the bottom,
into your small intestine,
a tube roughly six metres long, all folded up.
Waves of muscle push it along, like toothpaste in a tube.
And between meals, your gut runs a cleanup cycle.
Every ninety minutes to two hours or so,
a strong wave sweeps the leftovers onward.
Scientists nicknamed it the housekeeper.
The gum rides along with corn skins, seeds and fibre,
through the large intestine,
and out with everything else, usually within a few days.
Chapter five. When it can go wrong.
So one swallowed piece is no big deal.
But doctors have reported rare cases in young kids
who swallowed gum every day, piece after piece.
The gum clumped together, sometimes with coins or seeds,
into a sticky ball that got stuck.
Those cases are rare, and they took a lot of gum.
So the best place for gum is still the bin, not your belly.
The seven-year myth is wrong,
but it's wrong in an interesting way.
Your body can't break gum down.
It simply moves it along, and shows it the door.
