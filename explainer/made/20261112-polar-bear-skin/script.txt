If you shaved a polar bear,
you'd find black skin.
And zero white hairs. Why?
Each hair is clear,
like a tiny plastic straw
with a hollow core full of air.
There's no white pigment in it.
So why does it look white?
Light bounces around inside those clear hairs
and scatters in every direction,
the same trick that makes snow look white.
Scientists once thought the hairs piped sunlight
down to the skin like fiber optics.
Turns out they don't.
Most sunlight never reaches the skin.
The black skin may soak up what little warmth gets through,
but the real heater is underneath:
up to eleven centimeters of fat,
plus fur that traps heat so well
polar bears almost vanish on heat cameras.
So is a polar bear white,
black,
or totally see-through?
