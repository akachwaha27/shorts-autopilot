In nineteen-oh-two, a president refused to shoot one bear,
and that's why your teddy bear exists.
Here's how it all started,
on a muddy hunting trip in Mississippi.
It's November.
President Theodore Roosevelt is out in the swamps,
on a bear hunt with the state's governor.
After days of searching,
Roosevelt hasn't seen a single bear.
So his guide, Holt Collier,
tracks one down with the hunting dogs,
and ties it to a tree.
Everyone waits for the president to shoot.
But Roosevelt says no.
Shooting a tied-up bear isn't fair,
so he refuses.
A few days later,
a cartoonist named Clifford Berryman
draws the moment for the Washington Post.
In his cartoon, the bear is small and scared,
with big round eyes.
Readers love it.
Berryman keeps drawing that little bear for years,
like Roosevelt's furry sidekick.
Now jump to Brooklyn, New York.
Morris and Rose Michtom run a small shop,
selling candy and toys.
They see the cartoon,
and Rose sews a soft, stuffed bear.
They put it in the window,
with a sign that says, Teddy's bear.
The story goes, they even wrote to Roosevelt
to ask if they could use his name.
The bears sell out fast.
So many people want one,
the Michtoms start a whole toy company, called Ideal.
But here's the twist.
At almost the same time, in Germany,
a toy maker called Steiff was making bears too.
Margarete Steiff started the company
by sewing felt elephants.
Her nephew Richard loved sketching bears,
and he designed one with arms and legs that move.
In nineteen-oh-three,
he took it to a big toy fair in Leipzig.
At first, almost nobody cared.
Then an American buyer ordered three thousand.
By nineteen-oh-seven,
Steiff was making nine hundred seventy-four thousand bears.
So the teddy bear was born twice,
on two continents, at almost the same moment.
Now look closely at a teddy from back then.
Long snout, low forehead, skinny arms.
It looks like a real bear.
But scientists who measured old teddies
found they slowly changed over the years.
The snouts got shorter.
The foreheads got bigger.
The eyes got rounder.
Teddy bears started to look like babies.
Why? Because people pick the cutest one.
Our brains are wired to love baby-like faces,
so the cutest bears sold best, year after year.
It's evolution, but in a toy shop.
Today, teddy bears are everywhere.
In two thousand, one Steiff bear,
with sapphire and diamond eyes,
sold at an auction in Monaco
for two point one million dollars.
And it's not just kids who love them.
In one British survey,
about a third of adults said they still sleep with a teddy.
All because, more than a hundred twenty years ago,
one president wouldn't shoot one bear.
