An electric eel can zap you with 600 volts.
But how?
That's five times your wall outlet,
and there's no battery inside.
Its body is the battery.
Most of that long body is packed
with special cells called electrocytes.
Each one makes a tiny charge,
less than a fifth of a volt.
Alone, that's nothing.
But they're stacked in rows,
thousands of them, head to tail,
like batteries in a flashlight.
When the eel spots a fish,
its brain fires one signal,
and every cell flips on at once.
About six thousand tiny charges
add up to one giant shock.
Zap!
The fish freezes on the spot.
Here's the twist:
an electric eel isn't even an eel.
It's a knifefish,
closer to a catfish.
So if your body were a battery,
what would you power first?
