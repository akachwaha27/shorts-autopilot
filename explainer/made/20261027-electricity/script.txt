Up to seven hundred sixty-five thousand volts
flow through the biggest power lines,
yet your outlet gives you just a hundred twenty.
Here's how.
It all starts with a spinning magnet,
maybe hundreds of miles away.
So let's follow the trip, in simple words.
Stop one: the power plant.
Almost every power plant does the same job.
It spins something.
Coal, gas and nuclear plants boil water into steam.
Dams use falling water.
Wind farms use, well, wind.
All of it pushes the blades of a turbine,
and the turbine spins a giant magnet
inside coils of copper wire.
Here's the trick, found by Michael Faraday back in 1831.
When a magnet moves past a wire,
it shoves the electrons inside that wire.
Spin it nonstop, and you get a nonstop push.
That push is electricity.
And because the magnet keeps flipping north and south,
the push swings back and forth too.
In the US, it swings sixty times every second.
In most of the world, it's fifty.
That's called alternating current.
Stop two: the voltage jump.
A generator makes about twenty thousand volts.
But right outside the plant,
a step-up transformer cranks it way higher,
up to seven hundred sixty-five thousand volts
on the biggest lines.
Why make it so extreme?
Think of a garden hose.
You can move a lot of water slowly through a fat hose,
or the same water super fast through a thin one.
Power lines work the same way.
Crank the voltage up,
and the current, the flow, drops way down.
Less current means less heat wasted in the wires.
Even so, about five percent of the power
is lost as heat along the way.
Stop three: the step back down.
Seven hundred thousand volts would fry your toaster instantly.
So the lines run into a substation,
where big transformers drop it
to a few thousand volts.
Those smaller lines run down your street.
Then one last transformer,
that gray can on the pole, or a green box on the lawn,
brings it down to what your house uses:
a hundred twenty volts in the US,
around two hundred thirty in much of the world.
Stop four: your walls.
The wire enters through a meter,
which counts every bit you use,
and then a breaker panel,
which cuts the power if a wire gets too hot.
From there, cables run to every outlet.
And those holes aren't random.
On a US outlet, the short slot is hot.
That's where the push comes in.
The tall slot is neutral,
the path back.
And the round hole is ground,
a safety exit if something goes wrong.
Now the wildest part.
Flip a switch, and the light turns on instantly.
But the electrons in the wire barely move.
They crawl slower than a snail,
mostly just wiggling back and forth.
What races through the wire is the push itself,
moving at close to the speed of light.
So the energy in your charger left that spinning magnet
a split second ago.
Twenty thousand volts, then seven hundred sixty-five thousand,
then down to the wall,
all in the blink of an eye.
