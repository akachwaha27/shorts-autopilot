Why do leaves turn orange in fall?
Here's the twist:
the orange was there all summer.
A leaf is packed with green chlorophyll,
the pigment that turns sunlight into sugar.
But it's also holding yellow and orange pigments,
called carotenoids.
All summer, the green drowns them out.
Then the nights get longer,
and the tree shuts the leaf down.
It stops making chlorophyll,
and breaks the old green apart.
Scientists think it pulls back about half the leaf's nitrogen,
saving it for spring.
With the green gone,
the hidden orange shows through.
And the reds?
Those are brand new.
Trapped sugars turn into red pigments,
right before a cork-like seal forms at the stem,
and the leaf lets go.
So fall doesn't paint the leaves.
It peels the green away.
