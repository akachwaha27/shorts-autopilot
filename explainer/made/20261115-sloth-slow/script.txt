Being slow is the reason sloths are still alive,
and you'd never guess why.
Sloths aren't lazy.
Their slowness is a survival plan,
and every part of their body is built around it.
Let's draw it out.
Chapter one. A diet with almost no fuel.
Sloths live high up
in the rainforests of Central and South America.
And they mostly eat leaves.
Leaves are tough, full of fibre,
and some are even a bit toxic.
They give a sloth very little energy.
So a sloth runs its body
like a phone on low battery.
Its metabolism is less than half
of what you'd expect for an animal its size.
And it has about thirty percent less muscle
than other mammals its size.
Chapter two. The slowest stomach.
A sloth's stomach has several chambers,
full of microbes that slowly break leaves down.
A full stomach can make up a big chunk of its weight.
Some scientists think one leafy meal
can take up to a month to pass all the way through.
That's the slowest digestion of any mammal.
And to save even more energy,
a sloth lets its body temperature drift with the weather.
On chilly mornings, it sunbathes to warm up,
like a lizard with fur.
Chapter three. The weekly trip.
Here's the strangest part.
About once a week,
a three-toed sloth climbs all the way down
to the ground, just to poop.
It digs a little hole, does its business,
and climbs back up.
That trip burns a lot of energy,
and it's the most dangerous thing a sloth ever does.
So why bother?
Scientists think it helps
a tiny moth that lives in its fur.
The moths lay eggs in the dung.
The babies grow up, fly into the trees,
and find a new sloth to live on.
Chapter four. A garden on its back.
Sloth hair has tiny cracks that soak up rainwater.
Green algae grow inside them,
so a sloth can look a little green.
The moths seem to feed that algae,
and the sloth may lick some off as a snack.
In one study,
a sloth carried about a hundred and twenty grams of algae.
Chapter five. Slow means invisible.
A sloth's biggest enemies
are harpy eagles and big cats.
Many of these hunters spot prey by its movement.
A sloth barely moves,
and it can look green and mossy,
so it blends right into the leaves.
To an eagle scanning the treetops,
a sloth just looks like part of the tree.
Even its body helps it hang around.
Its organs are anchored to its ribs,
so they don't squash its lungs when it hangs upside down.
And here's a bonus.
In water,
sloths are surprisingly good swimmers.
They paddle with those long arms,
often faster than they can crawl on land.
So sloths aren't failing at being fast.
They're winning at being slow.
In a jungle full of hunters,
saving energy and staying unseen beats speed every time.
Next time, how does your heart
beat about a hundred thousand times a day?
