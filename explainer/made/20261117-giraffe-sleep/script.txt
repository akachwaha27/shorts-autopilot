Giraffes dream for about eight minutes a night.
You'd beat that before your first snooze.
And yet the tallest animal on Earth seems totally fine.
So how does a giraffe get by on so little sleep?
And why does it sleep in such a weird way?
Chapter one. The tallest short sleeper.
You'll often hear that giraffes sleep about thirty minutes a day.
But the truth is a little more interesting.
In Namibia, scientists fitted three wild giraffes
with tiny motion sensors,
clipped onto the little horns on their heads.
For months, they logged every move,
all night long.
The giraffes were resting for around eight hours a night.
But the deepest kind of sleep, the kind where you dream,
added up to only about eight minutes.
Usually in two short bursts.
Chapter two. Sleeping on your feet.
So what are giraffes doing the rest of the time?
Mostly, they doze standing up.
Eyes half open, ears still twitching,
for just a few minutes at a time.
If a lion creeps closer,
a standing giraffe can run right away.
Lying down is a whole different story.
Chapter three. The neck problem.
Picture folding a five-metre animal onto the ground.
First the front knees bend, then the back legs fold,
Getting back up means rocking that long neck
to swing its weight,
and pushing up one end at a time.
It only takes a few seconds.
But with a lion nearby, a few seconds is a very long time.
So why lie down at all?
Because dreaming sleep relaxes the muscles.
A giraffe can't hold up its heavy neck while it dreams.
So it curls its neck right back, like a swan,
and rests its head on its own rump.
A pillow it never has to pack.
Chapter four. When and where.
There are two favourite times for a snooze.
A few hours after sunset, around nine to eleven,
and again before sunrise, around three to five in the morning.
Giraffes pick resting spots
with a clear view,
often in a small group, where more eyes can spot trouble.
And there's one more thing stealing their sleep. Lunch.
Giraffes eat leaves, and leaves are tough.
So like cows, they chew their food twice.
They bring it back up and chew it again, as cud.
But a giraffe can't drift into deep sleep while it's chewing.
Chapter five. Why so little?
Big plant-eaters tend to sleep much less than meat-eaters.
A lion can fill up on one meal and nap for most of the day.
A giraffe has to keep eating for a big part of every day,
and stay alert to the animals that want to eat it.
So it sleeps in tiny slices,
and only lets its guard down for a few minutes of dreams.
And honestly, that's kind of genius.
In zoos, where nothing hunts them,
giraffes lie down for much more of the night.
So the short sleep isn't a flaw.
It's a survival plan, built for the open plains.
So next time you hit snooze,
think of the giraffe,
dreaming for eight minutes, with its head on its own rump.
