Back in 2012, a team of scientists
set out to measure every crocodile on Earth.
All 23 living species,
from tiny caimans to giant gharials.
They wrestled each one
into biting down on a padded force meter.
The winner was the saltwater crocodile.
One big male bit down with about 3,700 pounds of force.
That's more than sixteen thousand newtons.
For comparison, you chew a steak
with maybe 150 to 200 pounds.
Lions, tigers and hyenas
land somewhere around 1,000.
So the saltie bites more than three times harder

So where does all that power come from?
Look at the back of a crocodile's head.
Those big bulging cheeks aren't fat.
They're almost all jaw-closing muscle,
packed around the hinge
and pulling the lower jaw up like giant rubber bands.
Now look for the muscles that open the jaw.
They're tiny.
A thin little strap
that just tugs the jaw back down.
And that makes sense for how a crocodile eats.
It lies still in the water,
then snaps shut and holds on.
Clamping needs huge force.
Opening only has to lift the jaw's own weight.
So evolution poured almost everything
into closing.
That's the trick behind the bare-hands myth.
Once the jaws are already shut,
the weak opening muscles have to push
against your grip,
and they lose.
Wildlife handlers use this every day.
They loop a strong rubber band around the snout,
or wrap it with a few turns of tape,
and the crocodile simply can't open up.
But please, never try this yourself.
Getting the mouth shut is the dangerous part,
and that tail is all muscle too.
Here's the most surprising part.
Those crushing jaws are also incredibly gentle.
Look closely at a crocodile's face
and you'll see thousands of tiny bumps.
Each bump is a touch sensor,
and scientists found they're more sensitive
than your own fingertips.
They can feel a ripple
from a single drop of water.
They help a crocodile sense prey in the dark,
and they let a mother crocodile
carry her newly hatched babies in her mouth

And then there are the teeth.
An alligator has about 80,
and each one can be replaced up to 50 times
over its long life.
A new tooth is always waiting underneath
the old one.
Scientists are studying how they do it,
hoping it might one day help people
regrow lost teeth.
So the crocodile's jaw is a one-way machine.
Record-breaking power to close,
barely any to open,
and a touch as soft as a fingertip.
