Every insect on Earth vanishes tonight.
What would you lose first?
The first thing you'd notice is the quiet,
no buzzing, no chirping, no crickets at night.
But the real trouble starts sooner than you think.
Let's walk through it, step by step.
First, how many bugs are we talking about?
Scientists think about ten quintillion insects
are alive right now.
That's more than a billion insects
for every single person.
Over a million kinds have names,
and millions more are still waiting for one.
So when they all disappear,
they leave a gigantic hole.
Start with the flowers.
Nearly nine out of ten flowering plants
need animals to carry their pollen,
and most of those animals are insects.
Bees, flies, beetles, moths and butterflies
move pollen from flower to flower,
so the plant can make seeds and fruit.
Without them, the flowers still bloom,
but most never turn into fruit.
About three quarters of our main food crops
get help from pollinators.
Apples, almonds, blueberries, cherries and melons
would become rare and very expensive.
And chocolate?
Cacao flowers are pollinated by tiny flies called midges,
about the size of a pinhead.
No midges, almost no chocolate.
Next come the animals that eat insects.
Most songbirds feed their chicks on bugs,
even the ones that eat seeds as adults.
One pair of chickadees
needs six to nine thousand caterpillars
to raise a single nest of chicks.
Bats, frogs, lizards and spiders
lose their dinner too.
In rivers, trout and many other fish
eat young mayflies and caddisflies.
Take away the bugs,
and the food chain crumbles from the bottom up.
Now for the gross part.
Insects are nature's cleanup crew.
Beetles and fly larvae break down dead leaves,
dead animals,
and poop.
Australia found this out the hard way.
When settlers brought cattle,
the local dung beetles couldn't handle cow pats.
The pats sat in the fields for months,
smothering the grass
and breeding swarms of flies.
So scientists shipped in dung beetles from abroad,
and about twenty-three kinds settled in.
Now picture that problem everywhere on Earth.
Fungi and bacteria would keep working,
but much more slowly.
So, would humans starve?
Not right away.
Wheat, rice and corn are pollinated by the wind,
so we'd still have bread, rice and popcorn.
But our plates would get very boring,
and we'd lose honey, silk,
and lots of fruits and vegetables.
Many wild plants would slowly fade away,
and the animals that eat them would follow.
Even the soil would suffer,
because insects dig it, mix it and feed it.
So the next time a bee buzzes past your head,
remember,
that tiny bug helps keep your whole plate full.
