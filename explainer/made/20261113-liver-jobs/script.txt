Chapter one. The biggest organ inside you.
Tuck your hand under your right ribs.
That's roughly where your liver sits.
It's a soft, reddish-brown wedge about the size of a football,
and it weighs around one and a half kilos.
That makes it your biggest internal organ.
And it's always full of blood, about a pint at any moment.
Most of that blood comes straight from your gut,
through one big pipe called the portal vein.
So everything you eat and drink passes through your liver first,
before it reaches the rest of your body.
Chapter two. The filter.
Think of your liver as a security checkpoint.
Inside, it's built from millions of tiny cells called hepatocytes,
lined up in rows like the pages of a book.
Blood seeps slowly between those rows,
and the cells grab anything that shouldn't be there.
Old medicines, leftover chemicals, and alcohol all get broken down here.
Your muscles also make a waste called ammonia,
which is toxic if it builds up.
Your liver turns it into urea,
a much safer waste that your kidneys flush out.
Chapter three. The bile factory.
Your liver also makes bile, a greenish-yellow juice,
up to about a litre of it every day.
Bile works a lot like dish soap.
It breaks big blobs of fat into tiny droplets,
so your gut can actually digest them.
Between meals, the bile waits in a little pouch called the gallbladder.
And here's a weird one.
Bile carries leftovers from old red blood cells,
and that's what turns your poop brown.
Chapter four. The pantry.
After a meal, your liver packs extra sugar away
as a starch called glycogen.
Then, between meals or while you sleep,
it turns that glycogen back into sugar,
so your brain never runs out of fuel.
It also keeps a stash of vitamin A, vitamin D,
vitamin B twelve, and iron, for later.
And it builds proteins your blood needs,
including the ones that make a cut stop bleeding.
Chapter five. The comeback organ.
Now back to that superpower.
Doctors can remove up to two thirds of a healthy liver,
and the rest starts growing right away.
The cells that are left simply divide,
and the liver can get back most of its size in a few weeks.
That's why living donors are possible.
A healthy adult gives part of their liver to someone who needs it,
and both pieces grow into full-size livers.
And scientists are still working out exactly how it knows when to stop.
